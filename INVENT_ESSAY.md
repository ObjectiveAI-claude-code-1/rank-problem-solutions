
When faced with a problem, we rarely lack for ideas. The real challenge lies in discernment — in looking at a collection of proposed solutions and determining which ones rise above the rest. This is the purpose of the rank-problem-solutions function: given a clearly stated problem and a set of candidate solutions, it produces a ranking from best to worst. It is, in essence, a formalization of good judgment.

The function accepts two inputs. The first is the problem itself, expressed as a plain text statement. This might be a business challenge, a technical question, a logistical puzzle, or a personal dilemma. The problem serves as the anchor for all evaluation — without understanding what needs to be solved, no solution can be meaningfully assessed. The second input is a collection of candidate solutions, each also expressed as text. These are the proposed answers, strategies, or approaches that someone believes might address the problem. The function's job is to examine each solution in the light of the problem and determine a relative ordering among them.

The use cases for such a function are remarkably broad. A product team might use it to prioritize competing feature ideas against a known user pain point. A student might use it to evaluate different thesis directions for a research question. An engineer might use it to compare architectural approaches to a system design challenge. A manager might use it to rank proposals from vendors who are each pitching a different way to solve an operational bottleneck. In every case, the core need is the same: there is a problem, there are multiple possible paths forward, and someone needs help thinking clearly about which paths are most promising.

To rank solutions well, the function must evaluate several interrelated qualities. These qualities form the philosophical backbone of the ranking and ensure that the function's judgments are grounded, consistent, and useful.

The first quality is correctness. A solution must actually solve the problem it claims to address. This is the most fundamental criterion and the one from which all others follow. A solution that is elegant, efficient, and beautifully articulated but does not resolve the stated problem is no solution at all. Correctness asks: if this solution were carried out fully, would the problem be resolved? Solutions that directly and accurately address the root of the problem must be ranked above those that miss the mark, address only a tangential concern, or rest on flawed reasoning.

The second quality is completeness. Problems are rarely simple, and the best solutions are those that grapple with the full scope of what is being asked. A solution might be partially correct — it might address one dimension of the problem while ignoring others. Completeness asks whether the solution accounts for the whole problem or only a fragment of it. A solution that resolves every facet of a multifaceted challenge should be ranked higher than one that handles only the most obvious aspect while leaving other dimensions unaddressed.

The third quality is feasibility. A solution that is theoretically perfect but practically impossible is of little real value. Feasibility considers whether the solution can actually be carried out given realistic constraints. Does it require resources, knowledge, or conditions that are reasonable to expect? Or does it depend on assumptions that are unlikely to hold? Solutions that are grounded and actionable should be preferred over those that are speculative or impractical, even if the latter are more intellectually appealing.

The fourth quality is clarity. The way a solution is expressed matters. A clearly articulated solution — one that is specific, well-structured, and easy to follow — inspires confidence and can be acted upon. A vague or muddled solution, even if it contains the seed of a good idea, is harder to evaluate and harder to execute. Clarity is not about style or polish; it is about whether the solution communicates a concrete, understandable course of action. Solutions that leave the reader knowing exactly what to do should be ranked above those that leave the reader guessing.

The fifth quality is efficiency. Among solutions that are correct, complete, and feasible, some will accomplish the goal with less waste, fewer steps, or lower cost than others. Efficiency asks whether the solution is appropriately proportioned to the problem. An overly complicated solution to a simple problem is a sign of poor thinking, just as a trivially simple response to a deeply complex problem is a sign of shallow thinking. The best solutions match the scale of the problem with an approach that is neither bloated nor anemic.

The sixth quality is robustness. Problems exist in the real world, and the real world is messy. A robust solution is one that holds up under variation — it accounts for edge cases, anticipates complications, and does not collapse at the first sign of an unexpected condition. Robustness asks: how brittle is this solution? Would it still work if circumstances shifted slightly? Solutions that demonstrate awareness of what could go wrong and build in resilience should be ranked above those that work only under ideal conditions.

These six qualities — correctness, completeness, feasibility, clarity, efficiency, and robustness — are not independent axes. They interact and inform one another. A solution cannot be truly robust if it is not first correct. Efficiency is meaningless without feasibility. Clarity supports every other quality by making it possible to assess them. Together, these qualities form a lens through which any solution to any problem can be thoughtfully evaluated.

The rank-problem-solutions function does not claim to replace human judgment. Rather, it aims to mirror the kind of careful, structured thinking that a thoughtful person applies when weighing options. By making the criteria for evaluation explicit and applying them consistently, the function provides a disciplined framework for a task that is otherwise easy to approach haphazardly. In a world overflowing with proposed answers, the ability to rank them well is not a luxury — it is a necessity.
